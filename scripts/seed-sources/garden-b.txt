[00:00:06] Interviewer: To start, how long have you had your plot?

[00:00:11] Devon: This is my first season, so about five months. I got the offer in February, which everyone told me was lucky. I had been on the list for a bit under two years. My partner put us on it and then forgot, and the email came at a point where we had genuinely stopped expecting it.

[00:00:41] Interviewer: What made you want one?

[00:00:46] Devon: Partly food and mostly wanting a reason to be outside that was not exercise. I work from home and I was noticing that I could go three days without leaving the building. A gym membership did not stick. I thought a plot might stick because things would die if I did not show up, which is a slightly grim motivation but it has worked.

[00:01:23] Interviewer: How has the first season gone?

[00:01:28] Devon: Mixed, and better than I expected in the parts I did not plan. The growing has been about half successful. The tomatoes did well, the squash did too well, the carrots were a complete failure and I still do not know why. I planted way too much in a space that turned out to be smaller than it looked in March. By July I could not walk between my own rows. What has gone better than expected is that I like being there even when there is nothing to do.

[00:02:19] Interviewer: What was the hardest part?

[00:02:24] Devon: Water, without question, and not for the reason you would think. The physical part is fine. It is that there is a schedule, and I did not understand the schedule for about six weeks, and nobody told me I was doing it wrong. I could tell something was off because people would go quiet when I was filling my cans. Eventually a woman explained it to me at the gate, quite kindly, and it turned out I had been watering on the wrong days the entire time. I was mortified. The information was on the corkboard, which I had never read, because I did not know the corkboard was where anything important lived.

[00:03:26] Interviewer: Was that covered in the orientation?

[00:03:32] Devon: It probably was. The orientation is one Saturday morning in March and it is about ninety minutes and it covers everything at once. Dues, the maintenance deadline, the workday requirement, the water schedule, the pesticide list, the gate code, the compost rules. I remember maybe a fifth of it, and I remember the parts that were about money because those felt consequential. Nothing was written down that I could take home. There is a handbook, apparently, and I found out about it in July.

[00:04:22] Interviewer: How did you find out about it?

[00:04:27] Devon: Somebody mentioned it in passing, the way you would mention something obvious. I asked where it was and got a slightly confused answer, and then a link the next day from someone else. It is a good handbook. It answers almost every question I spent the spring being confused about. If I had read it in March I would have avoided the entire water situation.

[00:05:10] Interviewer: Have you gotten to know other members?

[00:05:16] Devon: A few, and it took longer than I expected. I go in the evenings after work, which is the busy time, so there are always people around, and for the first two months not one of them spoke to me beyond a nod. I do not think that was unfriendliness. I think everyone assumed someone else had already welcomed me. The people I know now I met through the workday in April, which forced everyone into the same task for four hours.

[00:06:04] Interviewer: What was the workday like?

[00:06:09] Devon: Genuinely fun, which I did not anticipate. I was on a crew spreading wood chips on the paths with three other people, two of whom had been there over a decade. You cannot spread wood chips for four hours in silence. By the end I knew who they were and what they grew and which parts of the site flood. That was more useful than the orientation by a wide margin, and it was not designed to be useful at all.

[00:07:02] Interviewer: Have you had any conflict?

[00:07:07] Devon: Not directly, and I have been aware of conflict happening near me. There is a disagreement about the two raised beds by the gate that seems to resurface every few weeks. I do not fully understand it and I have deliberately stayed out of it, because I have been there five months and I do not think I have earned an opinion. What I notice is that nobody explains the history to you. You catch about a third of a conversation and you can tell there are years behind it.

[00:08:11] Interviewer: Does that make it harder to participate?

[00:08:17] Devon: Yes, and I think that is the main thing separating me from the people who have been there a while. It is not gardening knowledge. I can look that up. It is that every rule has a story attached, and the story is what tells you how seriously to take the rule. The maintenance deadline sounds harsh on paper. Then somebody tells you about a plot that sat abandoned all summer and grew a bindweed problem that took three years to clear, and the deadline stops sounding harsh. Nobody hands you that. You pick it up in fragments over a year.

[00:09:24] Interviewer: Will you renew for next season?

[00:09:29] Devon: Yes, and I was not sure in June. June was the low point. Everything needed water, the squash was out of control, I had a work deadline, and I went eleven days without going in. When I came back it looked like a failure, and I sat on the bench by the compost for a while genuinely considering giving up the plot. What changed it was that a neighbor two plots down came over and said something like, this is the month everyone quits, do not quit in the worst month. Then he helped me cut the squash back. Twenty minutes of his time and I am renewing.

[00:10:41] Interviewer: What would you tell someone starting next spring?

[00:10:48] Devon: Read the handbook before you plant anything. Plant half of what you want to plant. Go to the workday even if you have a reason not to. And ask a question early, because the longer you wait the more embarrassing it gets to ask something basic. I spent six weeks watering on the wrong days rather than asking a question I was afraid would sound stupid.

[00:11:39] Interviewer: Anything the garden could do differently?

[00:11:45] Devon: Send the handbook with the offer email. That is the whole answer and it costs nothing. Everything I was confused about was written down somewhere I did not know existed. There is a real gap between the information existing and the information reaching a person, and the garden treats those as the same thing.

[00:12:24] Interviewer: Thank you, this is very useful.

[00:12:29] Devon: Happy to help. Ask me again next year and I expect I will have completely different complaints.
